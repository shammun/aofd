The big idea in one paragraph

Proof-of-concept sample — one block of Chapter 0A, written in the Chapter-0 voice.

Vallis multiplies arrows on almost every page: the wind dotted with a temperature slope tells you how fast a place warms or cools, the wind crossed with the Earth's spin gives the Coriolis effect (on a spinning planet, anything moving seems to curve sideways: to the right in the northern hemisphere, to the left in the southern). 0.3 The dot product: how much of one arrow points along another¶

What is this section about? One way to multiply two arrows and get a plain number back. That number answers a very practical question — how much of this arrow points along that one? — and it sits inside almost every equation of Vallis, starting with the very first boxed one in Chapter 1.

🧩 The dot product¶

The question: How much of the wind is actually blowing toward the warm air?

Imagine a breeze and a warm town¶

Imagine you are standing in a flat field. A steady breeze of 10 m/s blows past you, heading toward the north-east (weather reports would call this a south-westerly, because forecasters name a wind after the direction it comes from). Somewhere to the north of you, the air is warmer.

Here is the question: how much of that breeze is actually carrying air toward the warm side? Not all 10 m/s — part of the breeze is going sideways, toward the east, and that part does not bring you any closer to the warm air.

Now picture a lamp straight overhead and a long line painted on the ground, pointing north. The breeze arrow casts a shadow on that line. The length of that shadow is the part that counts: here about 7 m/s (we will get 7.07 m/s in a minute). That's roughly a fast cycling speed — the other 7 m/s or so is going sideways.

The dot product is exactly this "shadow along a direction", made precise. That's the whole idea; the rest of this block makes it exact, proves it, and turns it into code.

Arrows, written as numbers¶

An arrow (mathematicians say vector) has a length and a direction. We write it in bold, like $\mathbf a$, and describe it by three numbers called its components: how far it goes east, how far north, and how far up.

$$\mathbf a = (a_1, a_2, a_3) = (\text{east part},\ \text{north part},\ \text{up part}).$$

These are the same three directions $x, y, z$ (east, north, up) that Vallis uses for a small patch of the Earth (📇 atlas); the arrows of length one along them are called $\mathbf i, \mathbf j, \mathbf k$.

Three more symbols, each the moment we need it:

  • $|\mathbf a|$ — the length of $\mathbf a$ (same unit as its components; for a wind, m/s).
  • $\mathbf b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ — a second arrow, the direction we measure along (in the story: north).
  • $\theta$ — the angle between the two arrows, somewhere from 0° (same direction) to 180° (opposite).

⚠️ Which convention? In this block only, $\theta$ means "the angle between two arrows". In Vallis, $\theta$ is potential temperature (a temperature-like quantity you will meet in Chapter 1) and latitude is the curly $\vartheta$; Kundu uses $\theta$ for latitude in his Chapter 13 and for colatitude (the angle down from the North Pole, 90° minus the latitude) in Appendix B. And Vallis's $a$ (not bold) is the Earth's radius, 6371 km — our bold $\mathbf a$ is just "some arrow".

📎 Primer — length from components (Pythagoras in 3-D). An arrow that goes $a_1$ east, $a_2$ north and $a_3$ up has length $|\mathbf a| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}$ — Pythagoras, used twice (once on the floor, once up the wall). For $\mathbf a = (2, 1, 0)$ that is $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$.

What you see. Two arrows from the same corner: a (teal) and b (orange), 45° apart. Light falls at right angles onto b's line (dashed ray, with the little square showing the right angle). The thick teal stripe is a's shadow on that line, 1.581 long (drawn a hair to the side of b's line so the orange arrow does not cover it).

How to read it. Both axes are components (east and north), drawn at the same scale so angles are true. The shadow starts at the origin and ends at the point $(0.5,\ 1.5)$, where the dashed ray meets b's line. Multiply the shadow by b's length 3.162 and you get 5 — the dot product, in the title.

What would change if… Turn b to (−1, 2), at right angles to a: the ray drops straight onto the origin and the shadow shrinks to a point, so a·b = 0. Turn it further, past 90°, and the shadow lands behind the origin — a negative dot product. (Try it in the explainer below with the perpendicular and anti-parallel presets.)

The precise version¶

Here is the exact statement. For two arrows with components $\mathbf a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$ and $\mathbf b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$, the dot product is "multiply the matching parts and add them up":

$$ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 = \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_ib_i \equiv a_ib_i \qquad \text{(K 2.2)} $$

(The last form, $a_ib_i$, is Kundu's shorthand: a repeated letter $i$ means "add over $i = 1, 2, 3$".)

And the claim of this block — which we will prove in a moment — is that this recipe gives the shadow picture:

$$ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\cos\theta \qquad \text{(0A.1)} $$

Kundu states (0A.1) without a number (§2.8); we label it (0A.1) so that we can refer to it. Dividing by $|\mathbf b|$ gives the shadow itself, as a length or as an arrow lying along $\mathbf b$:

$$ \underbrace{|\mathbf a|\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf b|}}_{\text{shadow length}},\qquad \underbrace{\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf b|^2}\,\mathbf b}_{\text{shadow as an arrow}} \qquad \text{(0A.3)} $$

((0A.2) is kept for the cross product's area, $|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b| = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\sin\theta$, in the next block.)

$$ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\cos\theta \qquad \text{(0A.1)} $$

📎 Primer — adding and subtracting arrows. To add two arrows, put them head to tail: walk along $\mathbf b$, then along $\mathbf c$; the arrow from where you started to where you ended is $\mathbf b + \mathbf c$. By components you just add matching parts: $(1, 3) + (1, -2) = (2, 1)$. To subtract, $\mathbf a - \mathbf b$ is the arrow you must add to $\mathbf b$ to reach $\mathbf a$ — it runs from $\mathbf b$'s tip to $\mathbf a$'s tip — and by components you subtract matching parts: $(2, 1) - (1, 3) = (1, -2)$.

📎 Primer — law of cosines. For any triangle with two sides $A$ and $B$ meeting at angle $\theta$, the third side $C$ obeys $C^2 = A^2 + B^2 - 2AB\cos\theta$. At $\theta = 90°$ the last term vanishes and you are back to Pythagoras; at $\theta = 0°$ it gives $C = |A - B|$ (a flattened triangle). Check with a 3-4-5 triangle: $25 = 9 + 16 - 0$.

🧮 Derivation — the recipe and the shadow are the same number (Eq. 0A.1)¶

What we want to show. Show that "multiply the matching components and add" (K 2.2) and "length × length × cos θ" — the shadow picture — always give the same number, for any two arrows.

The plan.

  1. Build a triangle out of a, b and the arrow from b's tip to a's tip.
  2. Measure that third side twice: with the law of cosines (geometry) and with Pythagoras (components).
  3. Set the two answers equal and cancel everything that matches.

Tools we use (each explained before this point): adding and subtracting arrows, head to tail and by components (primer above) · length from components (primer above) · law of cosines (primer above) · the rule (x − y)² = x² − 2xy + y²

We start from

$$ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 = \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_ib_i \equiv a_ib_i $$

In words: the component recipe (K 2.2). We assume neither arrow is zero; if one is, θ means nothing and both sides are 0.

Step 1 of 9 — Draw the third side.

$$ \mathbf c = \mathbf a - \mathbf b $$

  • Why we can do this: Head-to-tail addition says b + c = a, so c = a − b closes a triangle whose other two sides we know; a triangle is what the law of cosines needs.
  • In words: c is the arrow that runs from b's tip to a's tip.

Step 2 of 9 — Use the law of cosines on that triangle.

$$ |\mathbf a-\mathbf b|^2 = |\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf b|^2-2|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta $$

  • Why we can do this: The law of cosines holds for every triangle; θ is the angle between the sides a and b, which is exactly the corner at the origin.
  • In words: This is the geometry route to the third side: it knows the angle but not the components.

Step 3 of 9 — Measure the same side with Pythagoras.

$$ |\mathbf a-\mathbf b|^2=(a_1-b_1)^2+(a_2-b_2)^2+(a_3-b_3)^2 $$

  • Why we can do this: The components of a − b are a₁ − b₁, a₂ − b₂, a₃ − b₃, and a length squared is the sum of its squared components (primer).
  • In words: This is the component route: it knows the numbers but not the angle.

Step 4 of 9 — Expand one bracket.

$$ (a_1-b_1)^2=a_1^2-2a_1b_1+b_1^2 $$

  • Why we can do this: We use the school rule (x − y)² = x² − 2xy + y² with x = a₁ and y = b₁; the other two brackets work the same way with 2 and 3.
  • In words: Each bracket gives a square of a, a square of b, and a mixed term with a minus 2.

Step 5 of 9 — Expand all three and regroup.

$$ |\mathbf a-\mathbf b|^2=(a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2)+(b_1^2+b_2^2+b_3^2)-2(a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3) $$

  • Why we can do this: Addition can be done in any order, so we collect the a-squares, the b-squares and the mixed terms into three groups.
  • In words: Squares of a, squares of b, and all the mixed products together.

Step 6 of 9 — Recognise the three groups.

$$ |\mathbf a-\mathbf b|^2=|\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf b|^2-2\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b $$

  • Why we can do this: The first two groups are |a|² and |b|² by Pythagoras, and the last bracket is the dot product recipe (K 2.2) word for word.
  • In words: The mixed products are the dot product — that is where the recipe sneaks in.

Step 7 of 9 — Set the two routes equal.

$$ |\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf b|^2-2\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=|\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf b|^2-2|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta $$

  • Why we can do this: Step 2 and step 6 both describe |a − b|², the length of the same side, so their right-hand sides must be equal.
  • In words: One side of one triangle, described in two languages.

Step 8 of 9 — Cancel the matching squares.

$$ -2\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=-2|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta $$

  • Why we can do this: Subtracting |a|² + |b|² from both sides keeps the equation true and removes everything the two routes already agree on.
  • In words: Only the mixed part was ever different between the two descriptions.

Step 9 of 9 — Divide both sides by −2.

$$ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta $$

  • Why we can do this: Dividing both sides by the same nonzero number (−2) keeps the equation true and leaves the dot product alone on the left.
  • In words: The component recipe equals length × length × cos θ — that is (0A.1).

Result

$$ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\cos\theta\qquad (0A.1) $$

In words: multiply-and-add equals the shadow of a on b, times the length of b.

What it means. Because |a| cos θ is a's shadow on b's line, the dot product is (shadow) × |b|. So it is positive when the arrows lean the same way (θ < 90°), zero when they are at right angles, and negative when they lean against each other (θ > 90°). It fails to mean anything only when an arrow has zero length (no direction). Watch out: θ is the angle between the arrows (0° to 180°), not the angle of either arrow from the east axis.

Check it. Units: a·b carries (unit of a) × (unit of b) on both sides. Limits: a = b gives θ = 0 and a·a = |a|² (Pythagoras again); i·j = 0·1 + 1·0 + 0·0 = 0 and cos 90° = 0. Numbers: for a = (2, 1, 0), b = (1, 3, 0): 2·1 + 1·3 = 5, and √5 · √10 · cos 45° = 7.071 × 0.7071 = 5 (worked below). The flattened triangles at θ = 0° and 180° obey the law of cosines too, so no special case is needed.

import sympy as sp                                           # symbolic algebra: checks the moves, not just the answer
a1, a2, a3, b1, b2, b3 = sp.symbols("a1 a2 a3 b1 b2 b3", real=True)   # six components, any real numbers
a = sp.Matrix([a1, a2, a3]); b = sp.Matrix([b1, b2, b3])     # the two arrows
dot = (a.T * b)[0]                                            # (K 2.2): a1 b1 + a2 b2 + a3 b3
c = a - b                                                     # step 1: the third side
side_pyth = sp.expand((c.T * c)[0])                           # step 3 + 5: |a − b|² by Pythagoras, expanded
step6 = (a.T * a)[0] + (b.T * b)[0] - 2 * dot                 # step 6: |a|² + |b|² − 2 a·b
assert sp.simplify(side_pyth - step6) == 0                    # steps 3–6 are exact algebra
cos_t = sp.symbols("cos_theta", real=True)                    # cos θ as an unknown
la, lb = sp.sqrt((a.T * a)[0]), sp.sqrt((b.T * b)[0])         # |a| and |b|
sol = sp.solve(sp.Eq(step6, la**2 + lb**2 - 2*la*lb*cos_t), cos_t)[0]   # steps 7–9: solve for cos θ
assert sp.simplify(sol - dot / (la * lb)) == 0                # cos θ = a·b / (|a||b|): that is (0A.1)
print("D0A-PoC1 checked: |a−b|² expands to |a|²+|b|²−2a·b, so a·b = |a||b|cos θ")

📎 Primer — numpy arrays, element by element. A numpy array holds a list of numbers. a * b multiplies matching entries (first with first, second with second…) and gives a new array; .sum() adds the entries up. np.arccos turns a cosine back into an angle in radians and np.degrees converts radians to degrees.

🐍 From scratch — $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\cos\theta$ (0A.1)¶

In [8]:
# 🐍 From scratch — eq (K 2.2) and (0A.1): a·b = a1*b1 + a2*b2 + a3*b3 = |a||b| cos θ   (no fluidpy here)
import numpy as np                              # numpy only for arrays, square root and arccos
a = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.0])                   # arrow a: 2 east, 1 north, 0 up (a pure example, no unit)
b = np.array([1.0, 3.0, 0.0])                   # arrow b: 1 east, 3 north, 0 up

dot_loop = 0.0                                  # start an empty running total
for i in range(3):                              # visit the east, north and up parts in turn
    dot_loop = dot_loop + a[i] * b[i]           # add one matching pair (2·1, then 1·3, then 0·0): total 2, 5, 5

products = a * b                                # the same thing all at once: matching parts multiplied [2, 3, 0]
dot_ab = products.sum()                         # add them up: the dot product a·b = 5 (K 2.2)
squares_a = a * a                               # each part of a squared: [4, 1, 0]
len_a2 = squares_a.sum()                        # |a|² = a1² + a2² + a3² = 5 (Pythagoras)
len_a = np.sqrt(len_a2)                         # length |a| = √5 ≈ 2.236
squares_b = b * b                               # each part of b squared: [1, 9, 0]
len_b2 = squares_b.sum()                        # |b|² = 10
len_b = np.sqrt(len_b2)                         # length |b| = √10 ≈ 3.162
cos_theta = dot_ab / (len_a * len_b)            # (0A.1) solved for cos θ ≈ 0.7071
theta_rad = np.arccos(cos_theta)                # the angle whose cosine that is [rad]
theta_deg = np.degrees(theta_rad)               # the same angle in degrees: 45
print(f"loop: {dot_loop:.3f} | all at once: {dot_ab:.3f} | θ = {theta_deg:.1f}°")

loop: 5.000 | all at once: 5.000 | θ = 45.0°

What does the code above do?

  1. a, b — the two arrows as arrays of three components (east, north, up).
  2. The loop builds dot_loop one matching pair at a time — this is (K 2.2) read aloud.
  3. products = a * b and dot_ab = products.sum() do the same thing in one go (numpy primer above).
  4. squares_a → len_a2 → len_a (and the same for b) are the lengths by Pythagoras, one move per line; cos_theta is (0A.1) solved for cos θ; theta_deg turns it into degrees.
  5. The second cell asks fluidpy.geo.vectors (dot, norm, angle_between) for the same numbers and assert_allclose stops the notebook if they differ by more than one part in 10¹².

What does this show? a·b = 5, |a| = 2.236, |b| = 3.162 and θ = 45.0° from both routes — the numbers of the tiny example.

# 🐍 From scratch — eq (0A.3): a's shadow on b, as a length and as an arrow   (no fluidpy here)
shadow_cos = len_a * cos_theta                  # left side of (0A.3): |a| cos θ = 2.236 × 0.7071 ≈ 1.581
shadow_len = dot_ab / len_b                     # right side of (0A.3): a·b / |b| = 5 / 3.162 ≈ 1.581
scale = dot_ab / (len_b * len_b)                # how many b's fit in the shadow: a·b / |b|² = 5 / 10 = 0.5
shadow_vec = scale * b                          # the shadow as an arrow along b: 0.5 × (1, 3, 0) = (0.5, 1.5, 0)
leftover = a - shadow_vec                       # what is left of a after removing its shadow: (1.5, −0.5, 0)
check_perp = (leftover * b).sum()               # the leftover is at right angles to b, so this dot product is 0
print(f"|a| cos θ = {shadow_cos:.3f}, a·b/|b| = {shadow_len:.3f}, shadow arrow = {shadow_vec}, leftover·b = {check_perp:.1e}")

What does the code above do?

  1. shadow_cos and shadow_len are the two sides of (0A.3): |a| cos θ and a·b/|b|; scale is how many copies of b fit along the shadow.
  2. shadow_vec turns the shadow into an arrow lying along b — the end point $(0.5,\ 1.5)$ in the figure above.
  3. leftover = a − shadow_vec is the dashed light ray of the figure; its dot product with b should be zero (at right angles).
  4. The library cell repeats both with V.scalar_projection and V.projection and asserts agreement.

What does this show? Both sides of (0A.3) give 1.581 — the length of the teal stripe in the figure — and the shadow arrow ends at $(0.5,\ 1.5)$. The printed leftover·b of about 1e-15 is round-off: the computer keeps about 16 significant digits, so "zero" comes out as a few times 10⁻¹⁶–10⁻¹⁵. ⚠️ Common confusion: a·b is a number, not an arrow. So $(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\,\mathbf c$ is an arrow (the arrow $\mathbf c$ stretched by a number), but $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c)$ does not make sense — $\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c$ is already a plain number and you cannot "dot" an arrow with a number.

📎 Primer — temperature gradient ∇T. At every point, the temperature gradient $\nabla T = (\partial T/\partial x,\ \partial T/\partial y,\ \partial T/\partial z)$ is an arrow that points toward warmer air, uphill on the temperature map, and whose length says how many kelvin you gain per metre. If it gets 1 K warmer for every 100 km you walk north, $\nabla T = (0,\ 10^{-5},\ 0)$ K/m. Lines of equal temperature on a map (isotherms) run at right angles to $\nabla T$. (The symbol $\partial T/\partial y$ means "how fast T changes as you move north, holding everything else fixed"; Chapter 0A teaches it fully in its partial-derivative and gradient blocks.)

Where Vallis uses it: the wind carrying temperature¶

Back to the breeze and the warm town. In Vallis's very first chapter, the rate at which a moving bit of air (a fluid parcel) sees a property $\varphi$ change — temperature, say — is the material derivative:

$$ \frac{D\varphi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t} + (\boldsymbol v\cdot\nabla)\varphi \qquad \text{(1.7)} $$

Here $\boldsymbol v = (u, v, w)$ is the wind (bold italic in Vallis; for a flat wind $w = 0$ it is just the horizontal wind $\mathbf u$), $\partial\varphi/\partial t$ is the change you would measure standing still, and the last term is our dot product. Vallis writes it as an operator, $(\boldsymbol v\cdot\nabla)$, "acting on" $\varphi$; for a single number like temperature that is the same thing as a dot product with the slope arrow:

$$ (\boldsymbol v\cdot\nabla)T = u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial T}{\partial z} = \boldsymbol v\cdot(\nabla T). $$

So the last term is the wind dotted with the temperature gradient, $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T$ for a flat wind. Read with (0A.1), that is (wind speed) × (how steep the temperature slope is) × cos(angle between them). This is advection: temperature being carried by the wind.

If a parcel neither heats nor cools on its way ($DT/Dt = 0$), (1.7) says that at a fixed spot $\partial T/\partial t = -\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T$. A wind blowing up the temperature slope (from the cold side toward the warm side, $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T > 0$) keeps bringing in colder air, so the spot cools.

A number. Wind 10 m/s, at 60° to a northward temperature gradient of 1 K per 100 km: $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T = 10 \times 10^{-5} \times \cos 60° = 5\times10^{-5}$ K/s. A day has 86 400 s, so that is ≈ 4 | 4.32 K per day of cooling at a fixed spot (computed below). That's like going from a mild day to a noticeably cooler one overnight — tiny per second, but it keeps going as long as the wind does. Wind blowing along the isotherms ($\theta = 90°$) gives zero: no advection, however strong the wind.

And the breeze from the start? Its share toward the warm side was 7.07 m/s. With the same 1 K per 100 km, that is $7.07 \times 10^{-5}$ K/s ≈ 6 | 6.1 K per day — and note the sign: air flowing toward the warm side means air arriving from the cold side, so the spot where you stand cools (also computed below).

# 🐍 From scratch — the advection term u·∇T in eq (1.7), with Earth numbers   (no fluidpy here)
speed = 10.0                                    # wind speed |u| [m/s]
theta = np.radians(60.0)                        # θ of (0A.1): angle between the wind and ∇T (which points north) [rad]
u_east = speed * np.sin(theta)                  # east part of the wind: 10 sin 60° ≈ 8.660 m/s
u_north = speed * np.cos(theta)                 # north part of the wind: 10 cos 60° = 5.000 m/s
u = np.array([u_east, u_north, 0.0])            # the wind arrow [m/s] (flat: no up part)
dT_dy = 1.0 / 100e3                             # 1 K warmer per 100 km northward = 1e-5 [K/m]
gradT = np.array([0.0, dT_dy, 0.0])             # ∇T: points north, toward the warm side [K/m]
products = u * gradT                            # matching parts multiplied [K/s]
adv = products.sum()                            # u·∇T by components (K 2.2) [K/s]
len_gradT = dT_dy                               # |∇T|: only the north part is nonzero [K/m]
adv_cos = speed * len_gradT * np.cos(theta)     # the same by (0A.1): |u| |∇T| cos θ [K/s]
adv_day = adv * 86400.0                         # the same per day (86 400 s in a day) [K/day]
dTdt_here = -adv_day                            # (1.7) with DT/Dt = 0: change at a fixed spot [K/day]
print(f"u·∇T = {adv:.3e} K/s (components) = {adv_cos:.3e} K/s (|u||∇T|cos θ) = {adv_day:.3f} K/day")
print(f"→ the fixed spot changes by {dTdt_here:+.3f} K/day")

breeze_north = 10.0 * np.cos(np.radians(45.0))  # the opening breeze: its share toward the warm north ≈ 7.071 m/s
breeze_adv = breeze_north * dT_dy               # its u·∇T [K/s]
breeze_day = breeze_adv * 86400.0               # per day [K/day]
print(f"opening breeze: u·∇T = {breeze_adv:.3e} K/s → the spot cools by {breeze_day:.2f} K/day")

Two more places it hides. Kundu's Appendix B writes the divergence of a wind as a dot product with the $\nabla$ arrow, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = \partial u/\partial x + \partial v/\partial y + \partial w/\partial z$ (App. B §B.3, unnumbered; Kundu's $\mathbf u = (u, v, w)$ is the full 3-D wind, which Vallis calls $\boldsymbol v$, so in Vallis's letters this is $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol v$) — multiply-and-add again, with "multiply" replaced by "take the slope of". And the cross product, next block, gives Vallis's Coriolis term $\boldsymbol f\times\boldsymbol u$ in the horizontal momentum equation (how the wind speeds up, slows down and turns):

$$ \frac{D\boldsymbol u}{Dt} + \boldsymbol f\times\boldsymbol u = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla_z p \qquad \text{(2.55)} $$

Every symbol, in plain words:

  • $D\boldsymbol u/Dt$ — the acceleration of the horizontal wind $\boldsymbol u = (u, v, 0)$ as felt by the moving air itself (the material derivative of (1.7), applied to the wind) [m s⁻²];
  • $\boldsymbol f = f\mathbf k = 2\Omega\sin\vartheta\,\mathbf k$ — an arrow pointing straight up, whose length $f$ is the Coriolis parameter: $\Omega$ is the Earth's spin rate, ≈ 7×10⁻⁵ | 7.29×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹, and $\vartheta$ the latitude, so $f$ ≈ 10⁻⁴ | 1.03×10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ at 45° and 0 at the equator (Vallis writes bold f for $f\mathbf k$; in Chapter 0 we write $f\,\mathbf k\times\mathbf u$);
  • $\rho$ — the air's density, ≈ 1.2 kg m⁻³ near the ground;
  • $p$ — pressure, ≈ 10⁵ Pa (1000 hPa) at sea level;
  • $\nabla_z p$ — the slope arrow of pressure measured along a level surface (fixed height $z$): it points toward higher pressure, so $-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla_z p$ pushes air from high toward low pressure.

A dot product shows why the Coriolis term never speeds the wind up: $\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf k\times\mathbf u) = 0$, because the turned wind is at right angles to the wind.

Where next. Chapter 0A §0.4, the cross product, starts from its component formula (Kundu writes $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf e_1, \mathbf e_2, \mathbf e_3$; here in our letters)

$$ \mathbf a\times\mathbf b = (a_2b_3-a_3b_2)\,\mathbf i + (a_3b_1-a_1b_3)\,\mathbf j + (a_1b_2-a_2b_1)\,\mathbf k \qquad \text{(K 2.20)} $$

and its index form, where $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ (the alternating symbol) is $+1$ for $ijk$ = 123, 231, 312, $-1$ for 321, 213, 132 and 0 when two indices repeat, and repeated indices are summed:

$$ (\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_k = \varepsilon_{ijk}\,a_ib_j \qquad \text{(K 2.21)} $$

plus its length, the area $|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b| = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\sin\theta$ (0A.2). Vallis §1.1 for (1.7). On the Kundu site, §2.8's material is taught under §2.1 and C08, the alternating tensor.

📖 Words used in this block

  • Coriolis effect — Moving things seem to curve sideways on a rotating planet — to the right in the NH, to the left in the SH.
  • Potential temperature — The temperature a parcel would have if brought adiabatically to 1000 hPa; conserved without heating.
  • Colatitude — the angle measured down from the North Pole: 90° minus the latitude (0° at the pole, 90° at the equator).
  • Wind direction (south-westerly) — forecasters name a wind after where it comes FROM; a south-westerly blows toward the north-east.
  • Fluid parcel — a small blob of air or water followed as it moves, small enough to have one temperature and one velocity.
  • Advection — a property (temperature, salt, moisture) being carried along by the wind or a current; in equations, u·∇T.
  • Isotherm — a line on a map joining places with the same temperature; ∇T is at right angles to it.
  • Dot product / scalar projection — multiply matching components and add / the signed length of one arrow's shadow along another.

✅ What should have clicked¶

  • a·b multiplies matching components and adds them — (K 2.2) — and that equals |a||b| cos θ (0A.1): a's shadow on b, times |b|. Zero means at right angles, negative means 'leaning against'. You can prove it with one triangle measured two ways (D0A-PoC1).

What later chapters build on

  • Vallis (1.7), $\frac{D\varphi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t} + (\boldsymbol v\cdot\nabla)\varphi$: the advection term $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T$ is a dot product — wind along the temperature slope warms or cools a spot; wind along the isotherms does nothing.
  • Chapter 0A §0.4: the cross product and $f\,\mathbf k\times\mathbf u$, the Coriolis term of Vallis (2.55), $\frac{D\boldsymbol u}{Dt} + \boldsymbol f\times\boldsymbol u = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla_z p$.
  • Chapter 0A ∇ blocks: divergence ∇·u is a dot product with the ∇ arrow.
