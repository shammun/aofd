## s02_coordinates
Let's start with a flat map. To say where something is, we need a starting point, and some directions to count from. The starting point is called the origin. Let's put it at a weather station. Now draw one line through it pointing east, and one pointing north. These lines are called the axes. A weather balloon drifts three kilometres east and four kilometres north of the station. So we can write its position as the pair of numbers three and four. [beat] These two numbers are called its coordinates, and together they pin down one place on the map. Measuring along straight axes at right angles, like this, is called Cartesian coordinates, after the French thinker René Descartes.

## s03_cartesian_arrows
Each axis comes with a unit vector: an arrow of length one that points along it. We write x hat for the arrow pointing east, and y hat for the arrow pointing north. The balloon's position is three times x hat, plus four times y hat. Three steps of x hat take us three kilometres east, and four steps of y hat take us four kilometres north, which lands exactly on the balloon. We met arrows like these in the vectors movie of this series. Here's their key property: x hat points the same way everywhere on the map. Move the balloon anywhere you like, and the two arrows don't change. That's what makes Cartesian coordinates so easy to work with. But many things in the weather aren't laid out in squares. A hurricane spins round its eye, and a weather radar looks out in rings. For those, there's a better way to say where something is.

## s04_polar
Instead of going east and north, we can give two other numbers: how far away the balloon is, and in which direction. The distance from the origin is called r. By Pythagoras, r is the square root of three squared plus four squared. Three squared is nine, and four squared is sixteen, so that's the square root of twenty five: five kilometres. [beat] The direction is an angle, called theta. Mathematicians measure it from the east axis, turning anticlockwise as seen from above. In a right triangle, the tangent of an angle is the side opposite it divided by the side next to it: here the north side over the east side, four over three, about one point three three. The angle with that tangent is about fifty three point one degrees, so theta is about fifty three degrees. [beat] So the same balloon is at r equals five kilometres, and theta equals fifty three degrees. These two numbers are called polar coordinates. A weather radar measures exactly this pair: how far away the raindrops are, and in which direction.

## s05_polar_back
Can we go back the other way? Yes. Think of the right triangle with the balloon at its tip. Its long side is r, and its angle at the station is theta. The side going east is r times cosine theta, and the side going north is r times sine theta. Let's check. Cosine theta is the east side divided by the long side, and sine theta is the north side divided by the long side. For our balloon that's three fifths, zero point six, and four fifths, zero point eight. So five times the cosine of theta is five times zero point six, which is three. And five times zero point eight is four. [beat] So x equals r cosine theta, and y equals r sine theta. These two lines are all we need to switch between the two systems.

## s07_radians
Before polar coordinates can do their real work, we need one more building block: measuring angles in radians. Take a circle of radius r, and walk along its edge. The angle in radians is the distance you walked, divided by the radius. So the distance along the edge, called the arc length, is the radius times the angle. A full circle is two pi radians, because its edge is two pi times the radius. Two pi is about six point two eight, so one radian is three hundred and sixty degrees divided by six point two eight: about fifty seven degrees. Let's try easy numbers. On a ring five kilometres from the station, turn by zero point two radians: that's about eleven and a half degrees. It walks you five times zero point two, which is one kilometre. [beat] On a ring fifty kilometres out, the same turn walks you ten kilometres. [beat] The same angle gives ten times the distance. Keep this rule in mind, because it explains a lot about maps of the Earth.

## s08_r_hat
Polar coordinates come with their own unit vectors, so let's build them one at a time. The first is called r hat. It's an arrow of length one that points away from the station, towards the balloon. Here's how to get it. The arrow from the station to the balloon is three, four, and its length is r, five kilometres. Dividing an arrow by its own length keeps its direction, and makes its length one. So r hat is three fifths, four fifths, which is zero point six, zero point eight. We draw r hat at the balloon, because it tells us the direction there. [beat] Its length is the square root of zero point six squared plus zero point eight squared. That's zero point three six plus zero point six four, which is one, and the square root of one is one. The same works for any point. The arrow to it is r cosine theta, r sine theta, as we just saw. Dividing by r, the r cancels, so r hat is cosine theta, sine theta. [beat] Why is it called r hat? Hold theta fixed and let r grow, and the balloon slides straight outward, along this arrow. So r hat points the way r grows.

## s09_where_theta_grows
The next few minutes build theta hat one step at a time, for anyone who enjoys seeing every move. You don't need these steps for the intuition of weather, climate science or maps, so if it feels like too much, skip ahead to the time on screen. Now the second arrow, theta hat. Its job is to point the way theta grows. So let's do the same experiment, with the roles swapped. Hold r fixed at five kilometres, and let theta grow a little. The balloon slides along the circle of radius five kilometres round the station. Which way is that, exactly? While it slides, its distance from the station doesn't change. If its move had any part along r hat, the balloon would get further from the station, or closer to it. For example, with a part of a tenth of a kilometre along r hat, it would end up five point one or four point nine kilometres from the station, not five. So a small slide along the circle has no part along r hat: it's at right angles to r hat. That's why the edge of a circle always runs at right angles to its radius. But there are two directions at right angles to r hat: one turned anticlockwise from it, and one turned clockwise. Theta grows anticlockwise, as seen from above, so the balloon slides anticlockwise. So theta hat is r hat, turned by ninety degrees anticlockwise. [beat] To turn that picture into numbers, we need one small tool: a rule for turning any arrow by ninety degrees.

## s10_turn_90
Let's start with the two easiest arrows. Turn x hat, which points east, by ninety degrees anticlockwise, and it points north. So it becomes zero, one. [beat] Turn y hat, which points north, and it points west. So it becomes minus one, zero. [beat] Now take any arrow, with an east part p and a north part q. It's p steps along x hat, followed by q steps along y hat. If we turn the whole drawing by ninety degrees, both pieces turn together, and they stay joined, tip to tail. So the p steps east become p steps north, and the q steps north become q steps west. Steps to the west count as minus in the east part. p times zero, one is zero, p, and q times minus one, zero is minus q, zero. Adding the east parts gives minus q, and adding the north parts gives p. So the turned arrow is minus q, p. [beat] In words: swap the two numbers, and put a minus sign on the first one. Let's try it on the balloon's arrow, three, four. Swap them, and put a minus on the first: minus four, three. [beat] Is that really a quarter turn? Minus four squared is sixteen and three squared is nine, so its length is the square root of twenty five: five, the same as the balloon's arrow. And its dot product with three, four is three times minus four, plus four times three, which is zero. [beat] A zero dot product means a right angle, as we saw in the dot product movie.

## s12_arrows_depend
Now let's move the balloon round the circle, and watch both arrows. Due east of the station, r hat points east, and theta hat points north. Due north, r hat points north, and theta hat points west. Due west, r hat points west, and theta hat points south. So r hat and theta hat turn as the balloon goes round, while x hat and y hat never change. That's the big difference between the two systems: polar unit vectors depend on where you are. Keep this in mind, because these turning arrows will matter a lot in Part 2.

## s13_components
The next two scenes split a wind into its polar parts, number by number, for learners who want every step. If you'd rather keep the big picture, skip ahead to the time on screen; you won't miss anything you need for weather, climate or maps. Now let's describe a wind in polar coordinates. Suppose that at the balloon, the wind blows due east at ten metres per second. We'll call the wind arrow bold u. Its eastward part is called u, and its northward part v. Here, u is ten and v is zero. [beat] How much of this wind blows away from the station, and how much blows round it? The part along r hat is called the radial wind, u sub r. The part along theta hat is called the tangential wind, u sub theta. To find each one, we take the dot product of the wind with that unit vector, as in the dot product movie. ## s27_recap
To sum up, coordinates are numbers that pin down a place, measured from an origin along chosen directions. Cartesian coordinates use x and y, and their unit vectors, x hat and y hat, are the same everywhere. Polar coordinates use the distance r and the angle theta, with x equals r cosine theta, and y equals r sine theta. The arc length is the radius times the angle, in radians. R hat points the way r grows. Theta hat points the way theta grows: it's r hat turned by ninety degrees, minus sine theta, cosine theta. The parts of a wind along these two arrows are dot products: the radial and the tangential wind. Turning the axes by an angle alpha gives new coordinates for the same arrow, and the four direction cosines make the rotation matrix. Polar parts are just turned axes, with an angle that changes from place to place. Round Hurricane Ian's eye, they split the wind into about thirty five metres per second of spin and thirteen of inflow, so the air spirals in at about twenty one degrees.
