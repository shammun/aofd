## s03_train
> remake 2026-11-07: changed (see remake_2026-11-07_log.md).
Let's start with the simplest moving frame. Imagine you're on a train gliding along a straight track at a steady twenty metres per second. You walk towards the front at one and a half metres per second. Someone standing beside the track sees your speed and the train's speed added together: twenty one and a half metres per second. [beat] Why do the speeds add? Let's measure positions along the track. The person beside the track uses x, measured from the station. You use x prime, measured from the back of the train. The back of the train passed the station at time zero, and it moves at capital U, twenty metres per second. So after a time t, the back of the train is capital U times t from the station. [beat] Your distance from the station is that, plus your distance from the back of the train: x equals x prime plus capital U t. And both clocks show the same time: t prime equals t.

## s04_velocities_add
> remake 2026-11-07: changed (see remake_2026-11-07_log.md).
Next come two short derivations: why speeds add, and why accelerations don't change. They're for those who like every step. If not, skip ahead to the time on screen: you don't need them for the intuition of weather, climate or maps. Now let's get the speeds from the positions. In a short time, delta t, your place on the train changes by delta x prime, and the back of the train moves on by capital U delta t. So the change seen from the station is the sum of the two: delta x equals delta x prime, plus capital U delta t. [beat] Next, we divide by delta t. Delta x over delta t is the speed seen from the ground, small u. Delta x prime over delta t is the speed seen on the train, u prime. So u equals u prime plus capital U. [beat] With our numbers, your walking speed plus the train's speed gives twenty one and a half metres per second again. [beat] Switching between two frames like this, moving at a steady velocity without turning, is called a Galilean transformation, after Galileo Galilei. ## s05_accel_same
> remake 2026-11-07: changed (see remake_2026-11-07_log.md).
Here's the beautiful part. What happens to accelerations? Let's follow the same steps, one move at a time. First, at every moment, u equals capital U plus u prime. Next, a short time later, each of them has changed a little, and the sum still holds. So delta u equals delta capital U plus delta u prime: the change of a sum is the sum of the changes. [beat] Then we divide by delta t. Now let delta t shrink, as in the derivatives movie. That gives d u by d t equals d capital U by d t, plus d u prime by d t. [beat] Finally, the train's speed never changes, so d capital U by d t is zero. So d u by d t equals d u prime by d t: the acceleration is the same in both frames. [beat] For example, if you speed up from one and a half to two metres per second in one second, the person beside the track sees you go from twenty one and a half to twenty two, in the same second. [beat] Drop a ball on a smooth train, and it speeds up downwards by nine point eight metres per second every second, for you, and for the person beside the track. And since Newton's law says force equals mass times acceleration, the same forces explain the motion in both frames. If you skipped, here's what to keep: in a frame moving steadily in a straight line, speeds change by the frame's own speed, but accelerations, and so forces, stay the same.

## s06_parcel_accel
In a fluid, we follow an air parcel: a small volume of air that moves with the wind. In the derivatives movie, we found the rate that an air parcel's own thermometer reads. Capital D T by D t equals partial capital T by partial small t, plus bold v dot grad T. The first part is the change at one fixed place. The second is advection: the change from being carried to places where the temperature is different. The eastward wind, u, is just another number the parcel carries along, so we can put u in place of T. Capital D u by D t equals partial u by partial t, plus bold v dot grad u. The same goes for v and for w. [beat] So the air parcel's acceleration is a local change at a fixed place, plus advection. Now here's the train's lesson, written for a fluid. On the left is the parcel's acceleration, worked out by someone on the ground. On the right is the same parcel's acceleration, worked out by someone riding in a frame that moves at capital U, with primes on everything. They're equal, because the acceleration is the same in both frames. But each piece on its own can be different. Let's see that with numbers.

## s07_sliding_pattern

The whole pattern slides east at a steady ten metres per second, without changing its shape. [beat] First, ride with the pattern, at capital U, ten metres per second. To you, the pattern is frozen, so the local change is zero. ## s09_lopsided

## s13_tau
The moving frame also explains what a fixed barometer sees. Let's take Plymouth, on England's south coast. Picture the pressure pattern as a frozen map, sliding east-north-east with the storm. A barometer that never moves sees the pressure fall as the low comes closer, even if the storm itself isn't deepening at all. So the barometer's reading mixes two things: the storm arriving, and the storm changing. To pull them apart, we need two observers. The first is the fixed barometer. Its rate is partial p by partial t: the change of pressure in time, at one fixed place. Here p is the pressure, and t is the time. [beat] The second is a rider, moving with the pressure pattern at bold capital U. The rider's position, measured in the moving pattern, is x prime, which is x minus capital U t, as on the train. And the rider's clock reads tau, the Greek letter t. Tau is the same time as t, but we give it a new name, because it marks a different thing held fixed. Partial p by partial tau is the change of pressure in time while x prime is held fixed: riding with the pattern. [beat] If the pattern really were frozen, the rider would see no change at all. So partial p by partial tau is the storm itself changing: negative when it deepens, and positive when it fills.
